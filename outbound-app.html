Outbound App

Redesigning the driver pickup experience for Jitsu's last-mile delivery hubs — a new Scan to Collect flow and a proactive Parking mode that together reduce congestion and establish a tighter chain of custody for every outbound parcel.

Role Lead Product Designer
Timeline 4 Months
Platform iOS, Android & Zebra
Tools Figma, FigJam

Overview

The Challenge

At Jitsu's distribution hubs, outbound associates were handing parcels to drivers with no structured verification step. The existing app only surfaced small sort package locations — large sort packages were entirely out of scope, meaning associates had to mentally track two separate systems during every handoff. At peak shift starts, drivers and associates were in the same physical space at the same time, creating congestion at the small sort desk with no mechanism for proactive preparation.

Beyond congestion, there was no reliable chain of custody between the sort wall and the driver's vehicle. Missorted parcels were discovered late, missing packages had no standardized search protocol, and there was no event record of who handed what to whom. The goal was to redesign the Scan to Collect experience from the ground up — and introduce a second mode, Parking, that let associates proactively prepare loads and bring them to drivers waiting in the lot.

Research

Understanding the Users

I spent time shadowing outbound associates across morning peak shifts at two Jitsu hubs, then conducted follow-up interviews with associates, shift leads, and drivers. The findings split cleanly between the two user groups:

  • Associates manually cross-referenced route manifests against physical parcel locations scattered across multiple areas of the small sort layout — they had no way to know when they had fully cleared a location.
  • When a scanned parcel didn't belong to the route being collected, there was no prompt — the missort either went unnoticed or required a verbal escalation that disrupted the handoff.
  • Drivers arrived at the desk not knowing whether their load was ready. With no proactive preparation system, the desk became a bottleneck as multiple drivers queued simultaneously at shift start.
  • Missing packages had no standardized search process — associates had no access to a parcel's last known location or who had last touched it.

"I know the packages are in two or three different spots but the app just gives me one big list. I have to keep running back to check I didn't miss anything at the first spot."

— Outbound Associate, Contextual Interview

Process

From Wireframes to High-Fidelity

The core design challenge was sequencing the workflow so that every decision point — badge scan, route selection, location-by-location collection, exception handling — felt like a natural next step rather than a branch in a decision tree. I ran three rounds of lo-fi testing with active outbound associates on the floor before moving to high-fidelity.

  • Round 1 — Early wireframes grouped all parcels in a flat list sorted by route. Associates immediately flagged that they needed to see groupings by physical location, not route — "I need to know when I've finished a spot, not when I've scanned half a route."
  • Round 2 — Introduced location cards with per-location progress counters. Testing revealed that associates needed a clear visual distinction between an in-progress location and a fully cleared one — a progress bar alone wasn't enough; they needed a state change (color + checkmark) to feel confident before moving on.
  • Round 3 — Missort and missing parcel flows were tested in isolation. Associates found inline exception handling — resolving a missort without leaving the collection screen — significantly faster than a separate modal flow that had been in earlier designs.

Solution

The Final Design

The redesigned app operates in two distinct modes depending on whether staff are available — both modes share the same driver ID and route lookup foundation, then diverge based on who is doing the work and where:

01

Driver Badge Scan & Route Lookup

Associates scan the driver's badge ID using a Zebra hardware scanner or manual entry. The app instantly surfaces the driver's name, vehicle, and all routes assigned for pickup — including both small sort and large sort package locations. Associates select one or more routes to begin collection.

02

Location-Grouped Collection

Each physical location where parcels are staged appears as its own card with a live count (e.g., 5 / 23 scanned). As associates clear a location, the card transitions to a completed state with a checkmark — giving them unambiguous confidence before moving on. Missorted parcels trigger an immediate inline prompt to redirect the parcel to its correct location.

03

Two-Mode Handoff: Walk-In & Parking

When staff are occupied, drivers walk into the outbound area and associates use Scan to Collect at the desk. When staff are free, the Parking mode lets associates proactively stage and bring the full load — small and large sort — directly to the driver's vehicle in the lot, eliminating desk congestion entirely at peak shift starts.

Edge Cases

Handling Missorts & Missing Parcels

Two edge cases required dedicated flows rather than generic error states — both are high-stakes enough to warrant their own resolution path:

  • Missort detection — When a scanned barcode doesn't belong to the active route, the app logs a missort event and surfaces the correct destination location so the associate can immediately re-sort the parcel. The collection flow resumes only after the missort is resolved — preventing misdirected packages from reaching the driver.
  • Missing parcels — If a parcel can't be located, the app displays its last known position and the identity of the associate who last handled it. The searching associate can attest they have physically looked (a logged button tap), then remove the parcel from the driver's route so the rest of the handoff can proceed without holding up the driver.

Results

Impact

The redesigned Scan to Collect flow and Parking mode were piloted across two Jitsu hubs over a 6-week period. Results measured against the pre-launch baseline:

55%

Reduction in desk congestion during peak shift start windows

3×

Improvement in missort detection rate before driver departure

91%

Associate task completion rate in post-pilot usability sessions

Reflection

What I Learned

The most important design decision in this project wasn't a screen — it was the principle that completion confidence matters more than raw progress. Associates didn't just need to know how many parcels were left; they needed to know when a physical space had been fully cleared before they walked away from it. That shift from a global count to location-level completion states was what resolved the "did I miss something?" anxiety that had driven workarounds in the old flow.

If I were to extend this project, I'd explore push notifications that alert an associate's device when a driver's expected arrival window is approaching — so the Parking mode preparation can begin before the driver is even on site, further absorbing congestion before it forms. I'd also invest in the dispatcher companion view to give shift leads visibility into which drivers have been handed off and which are still waiting.
